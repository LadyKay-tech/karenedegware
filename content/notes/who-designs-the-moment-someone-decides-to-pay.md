Imagine a team that has spent a year building a genuinely good product. The data is clean, the charts are elegant and every feature on the roadmap has shipped. In the final weeks before launch, someone raises the question nobody has really owned: how will this make money? A pricing page is drafted, a paywall is placed at the end of the trial, and the product goes live.

Now imagine the analyst who signs up on the first morning. She has a report due on Friday and a folder of spreadsheets she no longer trusts. She explores for twenty minutes, admires the charts, and still has not found the one answer she came for. On the third day she is asked to upgrade, and she closes the tab. The price was reasonable; she simply had not yet seen what she would be paying for.

The product had not failed her on features; it had failed her on timing. Nobody had designed the path from her problem to her first useful result, or decided what she should believe before the product asked for her money.

That moment rarely has an owner. Design owns the screens, engineering owns the build, sales owns the deal and finance owns the price, so the decision to pay usually lands wherever the build happened to finish. I have come to see it as the most important experience a product creates, and one that deserves to be designed as deliberately as any screen.

So before I ask what a product should charge, I ask something else: what should a user have experienced by the time we ask them to pay?
