Tiqki.

Tiqki came out of watching how much of a small business’s day disappears into managing a queue. A clinic or a salon would have a room full of people, a receptionist trying to remember who arrived first, and customers who could not step outside for five minutes without worrying they would lose their place. Tiqki gave both sides something better. Customers scan a QR code at the door, join the queue on their own phone and watch their place move, and when it is their turn the page changes and a text arrives.

Behind the desk, staff see walk-ins and booked appointments in one list and call the next person with a single tap. Over time the dashboard builds up a picture of wait times across the day, which lets a business plan its staff around the busy hours rather than guess at them. Tiqki has since been sold. The buyer took the product, and the Tiqki name stayed with me.

Decisions that shaped it

  • I kept it free of downloads. Asking someone to install an app so they can wait ten minutes loses most of them, while a QR code and a web page on their own phone loses almost nobody.
  • Walk-ins and bookings live in the same list, so the desk works from one view and a booked customer never gets stuck behind the people who simply walked in.
  • The text message mattered more than any screen. Once people trusted they would be told when it was their turn, they stepped out for a coffee instead of hovering by the desk, and the wait felt shorter than it really was.
